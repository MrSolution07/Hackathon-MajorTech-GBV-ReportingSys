TECHNOLOGY-FACILITATED GENDER-BASED VIOLENCE AGAINST WOMEN AND GIRLS
1. Introduction
Technology has become an important part of everyday life. People use smartphones, social media, messaging applications and other digital platforms to communicate, study, work, run businesses and share information. However, these technologies can also be misused to threaten, harass, control, embarrass and exploit other people. Women and girls are particularly affected by forms of online abuse that are connected to gender inequality and discrimination.
Technology-facilitated gender-based violence (TF-GBV) refers to harmful acts that are committed, assisted, intensified or spread through digital technologies and that are connected to gender-based discrimination or unequal power relationships. This type of violence can occur through social media, messaging applications, websites, online gaming platforms and other digital communication channels (Hartmann et al., 2025).
Unlike some forms of violence that occur in physical spaces, online abuse can reach a large audience within a short period. Harmful content can be copied, shared repeatedly and remain accessible even after the original post has been deleted. Victims may also face abuse from anonymous accounts or receive threats from people who know their personal information.
Technology-facilitated gender-based violence is therefore not simply a problem of inappropriate online behaviour. It can affect a person's safety, privacy, emotional wellbeing, education, employment and participation in public life. Addressing this problem requires a combination of education, appropriate support services, effective reporting systems, responsible technology and improved responses from online platforms.
This research examines the main forms of technology-facilitated gender-based violence, its impact on women and girls, evidence from South Africa and the wider world, existing responses and the opportunities for developing an AI-supported solution.
2. Understanding Technology-Facilitated Gender-Based Violence
Technology-facilitated gender-based violence occurs when digital tools are used to commit or intensify abuse connected to gender. It may be committed by strangers, acquaintances, intimate partners, former partners, colleagues or other individuals.
The term includes more than offensive comments on social media. It covers different behaviours that can threaten a person's dignity, privacy, freedom and safety.
The main forms relevant to this research are online harassment, image-based abuse and deepfake abuse.
2.1 Online harassment and cyberbullying
Online harassment involves repeated or serious unwanted behaviour through digital platforms. Examples include sending threatening messages, making sexually degrading comments, spreading humiliating rumours, targeting someone with abusive posts or encouraging other users to attack them.
Cyberbullying is a related term commonly used to describe bullying through digital communication. Although anyone can experience online harassment, women and girls may be targeted with gendered insults, sexual threats and comments intended to silence them because of their gender.
For example, a woman who shares her opinions online might receive repeated abusive messages telling her that she does not belong in public discussions. If other users join the harassment, the abuse can spread quickly and become difficult to manage.
2.2 Image-based abuse
Image-based abuse involves the creation, distribution or threatened distribution of intimate or sexual images without the consent of the person depicted. It is sometimes described as non-consensual intimate image (NCII) abuse.
The images may originally have been taken consensually, obtained without permission, or created or altered using digital tools. Having an intimate image does not give another person permission to share it.
An abuser may threaten to publish private images to force someone to remain in a relationship, provide money, perform unwanted acts or comply with other demands. This behaviour can cause serious fear and distress even when the images have not yet been shared publicly.
Once an image has been distributed, it may be downloaded, copied and reposted on other websites or messaging groups. Removing the original post may therefore not remove every copy.
2.3 Deepfakes and AI-generated abuse
Deepfakes are images, videos or audio recordings that have been generated or manipulated using artificial intelligence or other digital techniques to make them appear authentic.
In the context of gender-based violence, someone might use AI to place a woman's face into a fabricated sexual image or video without her consent. Such material may then be shared to humiliate, threaten or damage her reputation.
A victim may experience serious consequences even when the material is completely fabricated. Friends, family members, employers or members of the public may believe the content is genuine, while the victim may struggle to prove that it has been manipulated.
Deepfake detection is also technically challenging. Some manipulated content may be difficult to identify reliably, and detection systems can produce incorrect results. For this reason, a technology solution should not automatically label a person or image as fraudulent or abusive based only on an AI prediction.
2.4 Doxxing, impersonation and cyberstalking
Other relevant forms of technology-facilitated abuse include doxxing, impersonation and cyberstalking.
Doxxing occurs when someone's private or identifying information, such as a home address or telephone number, is published without permission in a way that may expose the person to harassment or danger.
Impersonation occurs when someone creates a fake account or uses another person's identity to deceive, embarrass or harm them.
Cyberstalking involves using digital technologies to monitor, contact, threaten or intimidate someone repeatedly. These behaviours may overlap with online harassment and image-based abuse.
Together, these forms of abuse demonstrate that technology-facilitated gender-based violence can affect both a victim's digital life and their physical safety.
3. The People Affected by the Problem
Although women and girls are the main focus of this research, their experiences are not identical. Age, disability, financial circumstances, race, occupation, location and access to support can influence how people experience abuse and how easily they can respond to it.
3.1 Adolescent girls and young women
Young people use digital platforms to communicate with friends, learn, build relationships and participate in social activities. Online abuse can interfere with these activities and make them feel unsafe in spaces they previously considered normal.
A girl who experiences harassment may stop posting photographs, avoid group chats or leave a social media platform. In some cases, she may also become afraid to attend school or social events because the abuse has spread beyond the internet.
3.2 Women entrepreneurs
Many women use digital platforms to advertise products, communicate with customers and grow their businesses. Online harassment can damage their professional reputation, disrupt customer relationships and discourage them from promoting their services publicly.
A woman who depends on social media for income may feel unable to leave the platform even when she is being abused. This creates a difficult situation in which protecting herself may appear to conflict with maintaining her livelihood.
3.3 Women journalists, activists and public figures
Women who speak publicly about social, political or community issues can be targeted because of their opinions and visibility. Harassment may include threats, sexualised insults, attempts to reveal private information and coordinated attacks by groups of users.
These experiences can discourage women from participating in journalism, activism and public debate. As a result, online abuse can affect not only individual victims but also freedom of expression and equal participation in society.
3.4 Women with disabilities
Women with disabilities may face additional barriers when trying to report abuse or access support. These barriers can include inaccessible reporting websites, communication difficulties, limited access to suitable services and discrimination.
Research on technology-facilitated gender-based violence does not always adequately represent every group. More inclusive research and accessible reporting tools are therefore important.
4. The Impact of Technology-Facilitated Gender-Based Violence
The consequences of online abuse can extend beyond the internet. Victims may experience emotional distress, social isolation, financial loss, reputational damage and concerns about their physical safety.
4.1 Emotional and psychological effects
Victims may experience fear, embarrassment, anxiety, sadness, humiliation or difficulty trusting other people. The repeated circulation of abusive content can make the experience feel impossible to escape.
The South African research discussed in the next section found an association between exposure to gender-based violence and symptoms of common mental disorders among adolescent girls. This highlights the importance of considering wellbeing when developing interventions (Hartmann et al., 2025).
However, an association does not by itself prove that online abuse caused every reported mental health symptom. Other experiences and circumstances may also contribute.
4.2 Social isolation
Some victims reduce their online activity, delete accounts or withdraw from online communities to avoid further abuse. While these actions may provide temporary relief, they can also limit opportunities for education, friendships, professional networking and self-expression.
The victim may feel that they are being punished for something another person did to them.
4.3 Educational and professional consequences
Online abuse can interfere with concentration, school participation and professional responsibilities. Students may become afraid of attending classes or participating in group activities if intimate images or humiliating content have been circulated.
For working women and entrepreneurs, online harassment may affect business opportunities, employment relationships and professional credibility.
4.4 Financial and physical safety risks
Some perpetrators use online threats to demand money or other benefits. The publication of private information can also expose victims to unwanted contact, stalking or threats in the physical world.
For these reasons, technology-facilitated gender-based violence should be treated as a safety issue rather than only a content moderation problem.
5. Research Evidence from South Africa
South African evidence is important because a solution designed for local communities must respond to the realities experienced by women and girls in the country.
A study by Hartmann et al. (2025), published in Frontiers in Global Women's Health, examined technology-facilitated and other forms of gender-based violence among adolescent girls in Gqeberha, South Africa.
The study included 1,540 girls aged between 10 and 19 from public primary and secondary schools in lower-income communities. Researchers examined reported exposure to violence during the previous year and its association with symptoms of common mental disorders.
The study reported that technology-facilitated gender-based violence was more common among secondary-school participants than among primary-school participants. Specifically, 43% of secondary-school girls and 11% of primary-school girls reported exposure to the study's measured forms of technology-facilitated gender-based violence during the previous year.
The researchers also found that girls exposed to both technology-facilitated and other forms of gender-based violence had a higher adjusted risk of symptoms of common mental disorders than girls who did not report those forms of violence (Hartmann et al., 2025).
These findings are significant because they demonstrate that technology-facilitated gender-based violence is relevant to South African adolescents and that it may occur alongside other forms of violence.
However, the findings must be interpreted carefully. The study focused on a particular sample of adolescent girls in Gqeberha. Its results should not be treated as a national prevalence estimate for every girl or woman in South Africa.
The study also demonstrates the need for more research on how victims experience online abuse, how they seek help, and which forms of intervention are most effective in different communities.
5.1 The South African legal context
South Africa has legislation relevant to certain forms of online abuse and harmful digital content.
The Cybercrimes Act 19 of 2020 addresses various cybercrime offences and includes provisions relevant to the unlawful distribution of certain intimate images. The Films and Publications Amendment Act 11 of 2019 also introduced changes relevant to harmful and prohibited online content.
The Protection from Harassment Act 17 of 2011 may also be relevant to some cases of harassment, depending on the circumstances.
The legal position depends on the facts of each case, and the existence of legislation does not automatically mean that every victim can obtain quick assistance or removal of harmful content. Reporting procedures, evidence preservation, platform cooperation and access to appropriate support remain important.
A proposed digital tool should therefore help users organise information and identify appropriate reporting options without claiming to replace the police, lawyers, courts or qualified support organisations.
6. Global Evidence of Online Violence Against Women
Technology-facilitated gender-based violence is not limited to South Africa. International research and reports have documented the risks faced by women and girls who participate in online spaces.
UN Women Australia summarises findings indicating that approximately 58% of girls and young women in a cited global study had experienced some form of online harassment. It also reports a UNESCO finding that 73% of women journalists experienced online violence in the course of their work (UN Women Australia, n.d.).
These figures illustrate that online abuse can affect both young women and those whose work requires them to communicate publicly.
However, the figures come from different studies, populations and research methods. They should not be combined as if they describe the same group, and they should not be presented as the current prevalence rate for all women worldwide.
The wider evidence points to a common concern: digital technologies create opportunities for education, employment and participation, but they can also be used to threaten women, circulate harmful material and discourage participation in public life.
International research also highlights the need to understand how online abuse intersects with existing inequalities. Women who experience discrimination because of disability, race, occupation or other circumstances may encounter additional barriers to protection and support.
7. Existing Solutions and Their Limitations
Several approaches already exist to address online abuse. These include platform reporting systems, content moderation, law enforcement, support organisations, digital safety education and specialised tools for preventing the spread of intimate images.
Although these approaches are important, they do not necessarily address every part of the problem.
7.1 Social media reporting systems
Many online platforms allow users to report abusive messages, impersonation, threats and harmful images. Platforms may review reports and remove content that violates their policies.
However, victims may find the reporting process confusing, particularly when the abuse involves several accounts, multiple posts or content shared across different platforms.
A victim may also need to report each instance separately or provide information in a format required by a particular platform. A report does not guarantee that content will be removed, and a platform's response time may vary.
7.2 Content moderation and AI detection
Some platforms use automated systems to identify potentially harmful content. AI can assist with detecting certain patterns in text, images and other digital material.
Nevertheless, automated systems can make mistakes. A system may incorrectly flag harmless content, fail to recognise abuse expressed indirectly, or misunderstand language and cultural context.
A detection tool should therefore support human judgement rather than automatically determine that every flagged message is illegal or that every reported person is guilty of wrongdoing.
7.3 StopNCII.org
StopNCII.org is an existing service designed to help adults affected by non-consensual intimate image abuse. It creates a digital fingerprint, known as a hash, from selected intimate images or videos on the user's device. The original content is not uploaded to StopNCII.org as part of this hashing process.
The hash can be shared with participating platforms to help them identify matching content and take action under their policies (StopNCII.org, n.d.-a).
This approach is valuable because it can support detection without requiring the user to upload the original intimate image to the service.
However, StopNCII.org explains that its protection is limited to participating platforms. It cannot remove content from every website or every part of the internet. Its eligibility requirements also mean that it is not the right tool for every person or situation (StopNCII.org, n.d.-b).
7.4 Education and support services
Digital safety education can help users recognise abusive behaviour, protect their accounts and understand how to report harmful content. Support organisations may provide guidance, counselling, referrals and assistance with reporting.
These approaches are essential, but a victim may still struggle to organise evidence from different sources or understand which next step is appropriate.
7.5 The remaining opportunity
The purpose of this research is not to claim that no solutions exist. Instead, it identifies an opportunity to explore how existing approaches could be brought together in a simple, privacy-conscious and accessible tool.
A possible area for improvement is the process between experiencing abuse and taking action. Victims may need help recording what happened, organising relevant evidence, understanding reporting options and finding appropriate support.
A new solution should complement existing platforms and services rather than duplicate them unnecessarily.
8. The Problem That Technology Could Help Solve
The central problem is that a person experiencing technology-facilitated gender-based violence may struggle to recognise patterns of abuse, preserve relevant evidence, organise information and navigate reporting processes.
Evidence may be spread across screenshots, direct messages, social media posts, account names, URLs and timestamps. Some content may disappear when a user deletes a message or an account is suspended. Other content may be copied to different platforms.
Victims may also be unsure which details are important to record or where to seek assistance. This can make an already stressful experience more difficult to manage.
The challenge is therefore not simply to detect abusive content. It is to help people move from experiencing abuse to understanding their options and taking informed action.
A suitable technology solution should aim to make this process easier without putting additional pressure on victims, collecting unnecessary sensitive information or promising outcomes it cannot guarantee.
9. Proposed Solution: An AI-Powered Evidence and Reporting Assistant
A possible solution for the hackathon is an AI-Powered Evidence and Reporting Assistant: a web or mobile application that helps users document technology-facilitated gender-based violence, organise evidence and access relevant reporting and support options.
The proposed tool would focus on three related tasks: identifying potentially abusive content, helping users preserve relevant information, and guiding them through possible next steps.
The concept is a proposal for further development. Its effectiveness, technical feasibility and suitability for different users would need to be tested.
9.1 Main feature: AI-assisted abuse detection
The tool could allow a user to submit text from a threatening or abusive message for analysis. A natural language processing model could identify patterns associated with threats, sexual harassment, coercion, repeated unwanted contact or other potentially abusive behaviour.
The system could explain why the content has been flagged and allow the user to decide whether to save it as part of an evidence record.
The AI should communicate uncertainty. It should not automatically declare that a crime has occurred, identify a perpetrator as guilty or make decisions that could place a victim at greater risk.
9.2 Main feature: Evidence organiser
The application could help users create a structured record of incidents.
Depending on what the user chooses to provide, a record could include:
•	The date and time the incident occurred or was recorded.
•	The platform or communication channel involved.
•	The account name or other available identifying information.
•	The relevant message, screenshot or public link.
•	A short description written by the user.
•	Notes about whether the content was reported and what response was received.
The application could arrange these details into a timeline and generate an exportable report for the user's own records or for submission to an appropriate reporting channel.
The tool should preserve the distinction between information supplied by the user, information extracted from a file and AI-generated interpretations. It should not invent missing dates, identities or other details.
9.3 Main feature: Reporting guidance
After an incident is documented, the tool could display possible next steps. These might include the relevant platform's reporting process, appropriate local support services, or information about contacting law enforcement when necessary.
Where official reporting channels or support organisations are available, the application should direct users to verified information.
The tool should explain that reporting does not guarantee that content will be removed or that legal action will follow. It should also allow users to save a record without being forced to submit it anywhere.
9.4 Main feature: Privacy-conscious image support
For non-consensual intimate image abuse, the application could provide information about established services such as StopNCII.org and explain how image hashing works.
If the team develops an image-hashing feature, it should consider whether images can be processed locally on the user's device and whether only the necessary digital fingerprints need to be stored or shared.
A simple prototype should not claim that it can automatically remove intimate images from the entire internet. Any removal or matching functionality would depend on technical capabilities, participating platforms and their policies.
9.5 Main feature: Safety and support information
The application could provide practical guidance about account security, blocking abusive accounts, preserving evidence safely and finding relevant support services.
It should avoid assuming that every victim can safely confront the perpetrator, report publicly or immediately leave an online platform.
Users should remain in control of their records and decisions. Where there is an immediate threat to physical safety, the tool should prioritise appropriate emergency and local support information rather than relying on automated advice alone.
10. How Artificial Intelligence Could Be Used
Artificial intelligence could support the proposed solution in several ways.
First, natural language processing could help identify potentially threatening or abusive language. This could make it easier for users to review messages and recognise repeated patterns.
Second, AI could assist with organising incident descriptions and turning selected information into a readable summary. The user should be able to review and correct that summary before exporting it.
Third, computer vision or specialised image-analysis methods could potentially help identify certain forms of manipulated content. However, reliable deepfake detection is a difficult technical problem, and detection results should not be treated as definitive proof.
Fourth, language technologies could help make instructions and reporting guidance more accessible. Support for South African languages would be valuable, but it would require careful testing because language models may perform unevenly across languages and dialects.
AI should be used only where it adds practical value. Features that can be implemented more reliably through ordinary software engineering, such as secure evidence organisation, incident timelines and report generation, do not necessarily require AI.
A realistic prototype could therefore begin with a simple evidence organiser and a limited AI-assisted text-analysis feature, then add more advanced functionality if time and resources permit.
11. Ethical Considerations, Privacy and Safety
A tool designed to assist survivors must not create new risks for the people it is intended to help.
11.1 Privacy and data protection
Evidence of online abuse may contain intimate images, private conversations, telephone numbers, account details and other sensitive information.
The application should collect only the information necessary for its intended purpose. It should clearly explain what is stored, where it is stored, who can access it and how it can be deleted.
Where feasible, sensitive processing should occur on the user's device. If information must be transmitted or stored remotely, appropriate security controls should be implemented.
A prototype should not collect real intimate images or sensitive victim information merely to demonstrate that the system works.
11.2 User consent and control
Users should choose which evidence to save, whether to generate a report and whether to share that report with another person or organisation.
The application should not automatically send evidence to police, employers, family members or social media platforms without the user's informed consent, except where a clearly explained legal obligation applies.
11.3 AI accuracy and fairness
An AI system may misinterpret sarcasm, context, slang, local languages or messages that contain threats expressed indirectly. It may also incorrectly flag harmless communication.
The tool should explain that its assessments are not legal findings and should give users an opportunity to review its suggestions.
Testing should include different language groups and relevant communication styles to reduce unfair outcomes.
11.4 Avoiding further harm
A tool could unintentionally increase risk if a perpetrator gains access to the victim's device or account. For example, saved reports, notifications or browser history might reveal that a person is seeking help.
The design should consider discreet access, secure authentication where appropriate, careful notification settings and safe ways to exit the application.
The application should never require victims to contact or confront an alleged abuser to use its main features.
11.5 Protection of minors
Because children and teenagers may experience technology-facilitated abuse, the design must consider their safety, privacy and support needs.
A tool intended for adults should not imply that minors are eligible for every service available to adults. For example, StopNCII.org has specific age and eligibility requirements.
The team should research suitable child-protection and support pathways before designing features for minors. Sensitive cases should be handled in a way that avoids exposing children to further harm.
11.6 Legal and technical limitations
The application should not claim to replace legal advice, law enforcement, counselling or professional support.
It should also avoid promising that a screenshot or generated report will automatically be accepted as legal evidence. Requirements depend on the circumstances and the relevant process.
These limitations should be clearly communicated to users.
12. Why This Problem Is Suitable for a Hackathon
Technology-facilitated gender-based violence is a meaningful challenge because it combines a social problem with opportunities for software engineering and responsible AI.
The project could involve web development, mobile-friendly interface design, natural language processing, secure data management, evidence organisation and accessible user support.
It also offers a clear way to demonstrate the problem and the proposed solution to judges.
For example, the team could present a fictional scenario in which a student receives threatening messages and discovers that a fake image of her is circulating online. The prototype could demonstrate how she selects a sample message, records an incident, reviews an AI-generated warning, organises the available evidence and finds appropriate reporting guidance.
The scenario should be clearly labelled as fictional, and the demonstration should use invented messages and sample data rather than real victims' private information.
The prototype should demonstrate a realistic, limited set of functions instead of claiming to solve every form of online abuse.
Possible success measures include whether users can complete the reporting-preparation process, whether the generated report accurately reflects the supplied information, whether users understand the tool's limitations and whether the interface protects their privacy.
The team could also evaluate whether the AI feature identifies sample abusive messages accurately enough to be useful, while checking for incorrect flags and missed threats.
13. Conclusion
Technology-facilitated gender-based violence is an important challenge affecting women's and girls' safety, privacy, wellbeing and participation in digital life. It includes online harassment, non-consensual intimate image abuse, deepfakes, doxxing, impersonation and cyberstalking.
Research from Gqeberha demonstrates that technology-facilitated gender-based violence is a relevant concern among adolescent girls in South Africa. International findings also show that online harassment affects young women and women working in public-facing professions. Although the figures from different studies cannot be treated as directly comparable, they highlight the need for effective and inclusive responses.
Existing measures, including platform reporting systems, content moderation, support services, legislation and tools such as StopNCII.org, already provide important forms of assistance. However, victims may still face difficulties documenting incidents, organising evidence and understanding their options.
An AI-Powered Evidence and Reporting Assistant could explore one part of this gap by helping users identify potentially abusive messages, organise incident records, prepare reports and access relevant support information. Its design should prioritise privacy, informed consent, accuracy, accessibility and survivor control.
The proposed solution would not eliminate technology-facilitated gender-based violence or guarantee that harmful content is removed. Its value would be in making a difficult process more manageable while connecting users with existing reporting and support options.
Ultimately, technology should help women and girls participate in digital spaces more safely, confidently and freely. The most effective solution will be one that addresses a real user need, respects the rights of survivors and works alongside broader efforts to prevent gender-based violence.
